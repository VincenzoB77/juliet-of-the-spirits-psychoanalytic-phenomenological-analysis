Abstract

Juliet of the Spirits (1965) marks a decisive turning point in Federico Fellini’s artistic evolution, inaugurating a cinema in which interiority becomes the primary reality. This article offers a
combined psychoanalytic and phenomenological interpretation of the film, integrating Freudian repression, Jungian individuation, Husserlian intentionality, and Merleau-Ponty’s embodied
perception. Giulietta’s journey is read as a confrontation with unconscious contents—desire,
guilt, childhood trauma, symbolic figures—and as a phenomenological reconfiguration of lived experience. Through its unprecedented chromatic language, dream imagery, and fluid boundaries between fantasy and reality, the film constructs a cinematic world where
consciousness shapes the very texture of existence. Giulietta’s liberation emerges not from external resolution but from a new relation to herself, revealing Fellini’s cinema as a profound meditation on identity, perception, and feminine subjectivity.

Introduction

Federico Fellini’s Juliet of the Spirits (Giulietta degli spiriti, 1965) occupies a singular place in the history of modern cinema. As Fellini’s first feature-length film in color, it marks both a technical breakthrough and an artistic metamorphosis: a departure from the social realism of his earlier works toward a cinema of visions, fantasies, memories, and interior landscapes. At the center of this transformation stands Giulietta, a middle-aged woman whose suspicion of her husband’s
infidelity initiates not merely a domestic crisis but a profound descent into the depths of her own psyche.

Unlike traditional psychological dramas, Juliet of the Spirits refuses the stability of realism. The film dissolves the boundaries between external events and internal experiences, creating a
cinematic space in which psychological states become visible. Giulietta’s encounters with ghosts, childhood memories, eccentric figures, and dreamlike apparitions suggest the operation of unconscious mechanisms shaping her perception of the world.

At the same time, Fellini constructs a phenomenological universe. Reality is not presented as an objective, neutral field but as a lived world structured through consciousness, emotion,
memory, and embodiment. The viewer does not observe Giulietta from the outside; rather, the
viewer inhabits her experiential horizon. The film becomes a meditation on perception itself— on how the world appears when filtered through desire, fear, guilt, and imagination.

This article offers a combined psychoanalytic and phenomenological interpretation of Juliet of the Spirits. First, it examines the film as a representation of unconscious conflict, repression, symbolic figures, and psychic transformation. Second, it explores how Fellini constructs a phenomenological world in which subjective perception becomes the primary reality.

Ultimately, the essay argues that Giulietta’s journey unites psychoanalytic revelation with phenomenological awakening, presenting self-discovery as a process of confronting hidden desires and reconfiguring one’s relationship with the world.

2.  Federico Fellini: Biography and Intellectual Context

Federico Fellini (Rimini, 1920) stands as one of the most influential filmmakers of the twentieth century. His artistic formation emerged in post-war Italy, a cultural landscape shaped by
neorealism and the reconstruction of national identity. Fellini’s early collaboration with Roberto Rossellini on Rome, Open City (1945) and Paisà (1946) initially aligned him with a cinema
attentive to social reality. Yet even in his early works — I Vitelloni (1953), La Strada (1954),
Nights of Cabiria (1957) — autobiographical elements, symbolic motifs, and visionary
tendencies began to surface, foreshadowing the stylistic transformation that would define his mature period.

This transformation becomes unmistakable with La Dolce Vita (1960) and, more decisively, 8½ (1963). In these films, Fellini abandons the constraints of realism and turns toward memory, fantasy, dream imagery, and subjective experience. Juliet of the Spirits (1965), his first feature- length film in color, marks the culmination of this metamorphosis. The transition from black- and-white to color parallels a deeper shift: from a cinema of external reality to a cinema of
interior vision.

During the early 1960s, Fellini developed a profound interest in Jungian psychology. He began recording his dreams, studying archetypes, and exploring symbolic imagery. As biographer Tullio Kezich notes, Fellini regarded dreams as “privileged manifestations of hidden psychic realities.” This fascination was not isolated; it emerged within a broader European climate marked by the decline of traditional religious certainties, the rise of psychoanalysis, and the influence of
existential philosophy. Juliet of the Spirits is a product of this intellectual milieu, where psychological depth and spiritual inquiry converge.

The casting of Giulietta Masina — Fellini’s wife and long-time collaborator — adds an additional layer of complexity. Masina’s expressive sensitivity provides the emotional and
phenomenological center of the film. Her face becomes the surface upon which Fellini inscribes the interior drama of feminine subjectivity.

Thus, Juliet of the Spirits emerges at the intersection of several cultural currents:

·         the dissolution of neorealist aesthetics,

·         the ascent of psychoanalytic thought,

·         the centrality of subjective experience,

·         and the search for a cinematic language capable of representing interior life.

Within this context, the film becomes a psychological and phenomenological laboratory — a space where Fellini experiments with the visualization of consciousness itself.

3.  Integrated Theoretical Framework

The interpretive richness of Juliet of the Spirits emerges most clearly when psychoanalytic and phenomenological theories are brought into dialogue. Fellini’s film does not simply illustrate psychological concepts; it creates a cinematic environment in which unconscious dynamics and lived experience become visible. The following theoretical framework integrates Freud, Jung, Husserl, and Merleau-Ponty, combining the narrative sensitivity of the first document with the academic rigor of the second.

3.1  Freud: Repression, Desire, and the Return of the Repressed

Freud’s theory of repression provides a foundational lens through which Giulietta’s crisis can be understood. Throughout her life, Giulietta has internalized a moral system built on obedience, modesty, and self-sacrifice. Her marriage reinforces these structures: Giorgio’s emotional
distance and probable infidelity destabilize the fragile equilibrium of her identity.

Freud famously wrote that “dreams are the royal road to the unconscious.” In Fellini’s film, dreams, visions, and symbolic apparitions function precisely in this way. They reveal desires Giulietta cannot articulate, fears she cannot confront, and conflicts she cannot resolve. The film’s fluid transitions between reality and fantasy mirror the Freudian logic of dreamwork, where latent content returns in disguised form.

The contrast between Giulietta and her neighbor Susy exemplifies the Freudian tension between desire and prohibition. Susy embodies erotic freedom, sensuality, and resistance to
moral constraint — qualities Giulietta has repressed. Her simultaneous attraction to and fear of Susy reflects the ambivalence characteristic of repressed desire: what is forbidden becomes both threatening and alluring.

3.2  Jung: Individuation and the Symbolic Figures of the Psyche

While Freud illuminates the mechanisms of repression, Jung provides a broader symbolic framework for understanding Giulietta’s encounters with spirits, memories, and archetypal
figures. Jung’s concept of individuation — the integration of unconscious elements into consciousness — resonates deeply with Giulietta’s journey.

Jung writes that individuation “implies becoming one’s own self.” Giulietta’s visions are not pathological hallucinations; they are autonomous psychic forces seeking recognition. The spirits represent fragments of her personality: fear, desire, creativity, vulnerability, guilt, and intuition. They form a symbolic community that exists alongside her everyday life.

Susy, in Jungian terms, functions as Giulietta’s Shadow — the rejected aspect of personality that remains unconscious. Jung describes the Shadow as “a moral problem that challenges the whole ego-personality.” Giulietta’s fascination with Susy signals the beginning of integration: what she fears becomes a source of self-knowledge.

The film’s symbolic richness — its colors, costumes, dreamlike settings — reflects Jung’s belief that the unconscious communicates through images. Fellini’s cinema becomes a visual analogue of Jungian psychology.

3.3  Husserl: Intentionality and the Constitution of Experience

Phenomenology begins with Husserl’s insight that consciousness is always consciousness of something. Experience is not passive reception but active meaning-constitution. Fellini’s film embodies this principle by refusing to distinguish sharply between objective reality and subjective perception.

For Giulietta, memories, fantasies, and visions possess experiential reality. They shape her emotions, decisions, and sense of self. Husserl’s concept of intentionality helps explain why
Fellini presents these experiences without hierarchical separation: they belong to Giulietta’s lived world.

The film thus becomes a phenomenological exploration of how reality appears when filtered through guilt, desire, fear, and imagination. Giulietta’s crisis reveals the structures of
consciousness that ordinarily remain invisible.

3.4  Merleau-Ponty: Embodiment, Perception, and the Lived Body

Merleau-Ponty deepens Husserl’s insights by emphasizing embodiment. “The body is our
general medium for having a world,” he writes. Giulietta’s experiences illustrate this principle vividly. Her emotional states are inseparable from bodily sensations, gestures, and spatial relations.

Color plays a central role in this embodied phenomenology. Fellini’s chromatic palette — intense reds, luminous whites, shadowy blues — does not merely decorate the film; it organizes

Giulietta’s affective world. Viewers do not simply understand her emotions; they feel them through the sensory structure of the film.
Space, too, becomes an extension of Giulietta’s psyche. Rooms, corridors, gardens, and dream landscapes are not neutral environments but lived spaces charged with symbolic meaning. The house becomes a body; the body becomes a house. This fusion of interiority and environment exemplifies Merleau-Ponty’s notion that perception is always embodied and meaningful.
 
3.5 Bumbalo's Analytical Phenomenological Psychology: Adult Ordinariness and Transformative Rupture
 
While Freud explains repression, Jung symbolic integration, Husserl intentional constitution, and
Merleau-Ponty embodied perception, Bumbalo's Analytical Phenomenological Psychology (APP) provides a higher-order integrative framework capable of unifying these dimensions within a single developmental process.
 
According to Bumbalo's Theory of Adult Ordinariness, adulthood is normally structured through what appears familiar, repetitive, psychologically secure, and socially validated. Ordinary life is not merely a neutral condition but an organizing structure through which identity maintains coherence. Individuals build their sense of self around routines, relationships, beliefs, and predictable meanings that gradually become invisible precisely because of their familiarity.
 
From this perspective, Giulietta's suffering originates not primarily in her husband's infidelity, nor exclusively in unconscious repression, but in the collapse of the ordinary world upon which her identity has been constructed. Giorgio's betrayal functions as an existential fracture that destabilizes the entire architecture of everyday normality.
 
What psychoanalysis identifies as the return of the repressed and phenomenology describes as a crisis of lived experience are interpreted within APP as consequences of a more fundamental process: the disintegration of adult ordinariness.
 
The ordinary therefore becomes simultaneously protective and imprisoning. It offers stability but may also conceal undeveloped aspects of the self. Individuals often mistake adaptation for authenticity, confusing psychological equilibrium with genuine self-realization.
 
Giulietta's visions emerge precisely at the moment when ordinary existence can no longer sustain itself. The spirits, memories, fantasies, and symbolic figures that invade her consciousness are not pathological intrusions. They represent alternative possibilities of being that become accessible only after the collapse of the ordinary framework.
 
Within the APP model, psychological transformation occurs through three developmental phases:
 

1.	Ordinary Stabilization.

2.	Rupture of Ordinariness.

3.	Emergence of Expanded Selfhood.

Giulietta's journey follows this trajectory. She begins trapped within a socially prescribed identity, undergoes a painful phenomenological and emotional disintegration, and ultimately discovers the possibility of a more authentic relationship with herself.
 
The central achievement of Fellini's film is therefore not the resolution of marital conflict but the cinematic representation of transformative rupture. Giulietta's liberation becomes possible only because the ordinary world collapses. What appears at first as crisis reveals itself as a developmental threshold.
 
APP thus reframes the film as an exploration of existential growth: the fall of ordinary certainty becomes the necessary condition for psychological expansion.
 
4.  Psychoanalytic Analysis

Giulietta’s crisis in Juliet of the Spirits unfolds not as a simple reaction to marital betrayal but as a profound destabilization of her psychic architecture. Fellini uses Giorgio’s probable infidelity as a catalyst: a single fracture that exposes the deeper layers of repression, guilt, desire, and
childhood conditioning that have shaped Giulietta’s identity. The psychoanalytic dimension of the film becomes visible through the symbolic figures, dream sequences, and emotional oscillations that accompany her descent into interiority.

4.1  The Marital Crisis as Collapse of the Self

On the surface, Giulietta’s suffering appears to stem from Giorgio’s emotional distance and suspected affair. Yet the film quickly reveals that her identity has long been organized around external approval, obedience, and self-effacement. Giorgio functions less as a partner and more as a psychological anchor — a guarantor of stability.
When his fidelity becomes uncertain, the symbolic order sustaining Giulietta collapses. This collapse resembles what psychoanalysis identifies as the return of repressed material: desires, fears, and memories that have been excluded from consciousness reappear in disguised form. Giulietta’s visions are not random hallucinations; they are psychic messages, attempts by the unconscious to reorganize a fractured self.

4.2  Childhood, Religion, and the Internalization of Guilt

Flashbacks reveal a childhood dominated by religious discipline, moral surveillance, and
emotional constraint. Giulietta was taught to associate sexuality with guilt and obedience with virtue. These early experiences form the foundation of her adult superego — the internalized moral authority that continues to judge, restrict, and punish her.
The accusatory voices, spiritual apparitions, and authoritarian figures that populate her visions symbolize this internalized system of guilt. They are not external spirits but psychic structures: remnants of childhood that still govern her emotional life.

Thus, Giulietta’s adult inability to assert herself is not merely a marital issue; it is the continuation of patterns established in childhood. Her crisis becomes an opportunity to confront these inherited constraints.

4.3  Susy as Shadow Archetype

Among the symbolic figures Giulietta encounters, Susy occupies a central position. Sensual, independent, and unapologetically erotic, Susy embodies everything Giulietta has been
forbidden to become. She represents freedom from moral constraint, liberation from guilt, and the possibility of desire without punishment.

From a Jungian perspective, Susy functions as Giulietta’s Shadow — the rejected aspect of personality that remains unconscious. Jung describes the Shadow as “a moral problem that
challenges the whole ego-personality.” Giulietta’s fascination with Susy reflects the beginning of integration: what she fears becomes a source of self-knowledge.

Susy does not merely tempt Giulietta; she reveals alternative modes of being. Her presence destabilizes Giulietta’s inherited identity and opens the possibility of transformation.

4.4  Spirits, Dreams, and Autonomous Psychic Forces

The “spirits” of the film’s title should not be understood literally. They are symbolic manifestations of unconscious energies. In Jungian psychology, dreams often present
unconscious aspects of the self as autonomous figures. Fellini translates this mechanism into cinematic form.
The spirits represent:

·         memory

·         desire

·         fear

·         eroticism

·         creativity

·         vulnerability

·         intuition

They constitute an alternative community that coexists with Giulietta’s everyday life. Their presence transforms the narrative into a symbolic pilgrimage toward self-understanding.

Giulietta’s encounters with these figures illustrate the process of individuation: the integration of unconscious contents into consciousness. Her liberation depends not on escaping these figures but on recognizing them as parts of herself.

5.  Phenomenological Analysis

While psychoanalysis reveals the symbolic and unconscious dimensions of Giulietta’s journey, phenomenology illuminates the structure of her lived experience. Fellini does not merely depict psychological states; he constructs a world in which perception, embodiment, memory, and
imagination shape reality itself. Giulietta’s crisis becomes a phenomenological event: a rupture in the ordinary experience of the world that exposes the deeper layers of consciousness.

5.1  The House as Phenomenological Body

One of the most powerful insights from the first document is the idea that Giulietta’s house functions as a phenomenological body. It is not simply a physical structure; it is an extension of her interiority. Rooms, corridors, staircases, and hidden spaces correspond to emotional states, memories, and unconscious tensions.

The house becomes:

·         a space of guilt (the childhood room),

·         a space of repression (the marital bedroom),

·         a space of desire (the garden and Susy’s world),

·         a space of fear (the corridors filled with voices),

·         a space of awakening (the final departure).

This topological reading aligns with Merleau-Ponty’s notion that space is not neutral but lived. Giulietta does not inhabit a house; she inhabits a symbolic organism that mirrors her psychic condition.

5.2  Color as Affective Structure

As Fellini’s first color feature, Juliet of the Spirits transforms color into a phenomenological language. Color does not decorate the film; it organizes Giulietta’s emotional world.

·         Red expresses erotic intensity, danger, and desire.

·         White evokes spirituality, innocence, and transcendence.

·         Blue and shadow convey anxiety, repression, and fear.

·         Gold and bright tones suggest fantasy, seduction, and imagination.

Color becomes an affective force. Viewers do not merely interpret Giulietta’s emotions; they feel them through the chromatic structure of the film. This sensory immersion exemplifies Merleau- Ponty’s claim that perception is always embodied and meaningful.

5.3  Crisis of Everyday Reality

Phenomenologists often analyze moments when everyday assumptions collapse. Giulietta’s discovery of Giorgio’s infidelity functions as precisely this kind of existential rupture. The familiar world of marriage, domestic routine, and moral certainty suddenly loses its stability.
What previously appeared natural becomes questionable. What previously appeared solid becomes fragile.

This crisis reveals the hidden structures of Giulietta’s lived world:

·         her dependence on external validation,

·         her inherited moral constraints,

·         her fear of desire,

·         her fragmented identity.

The visions, spirits, and memories that emerge during this period are not hallucinations but phenomenological manifestations of a consciousness attempting to reorganize itself.

5.4  Plurality of Realities

One of Fellini’s most radical achievements is his refusal to privilege objective reality over
imagination. Traditional realist cinema distinguishes clearly between real events and fantasies.

Juliet of the Spirits deliberately undermines this distinction.

From a phenomenological standpoint, this strategy reflects the complexity of human
experience. Dreams, memories, fantasies, and perceptions all contribute to the constitution of reality as it is lived.

For Giulietta:

·         a memory is as real as a present event,

·         a fantasy is as real as a conversation,

·         a spirit is as real as Giorgio’s betrayal.

These experiences possess experiential reality. They shape her emotions, her decisions, and her sense of self. Fellini thus constructs a pluralistic world in which multiple modes of reality coexist.
 
 
5.5 Nino Rota, Temporality, and the Acoustic Phenomenology of Adult Ordinariness
 
Within Bumbalo's Theory of Adult Ordinariness, Nino Rota's score in Juliet of the Spirits may be understood not merely as emotional accompaniment but as a phenomenological structure that reveals the temporal foundations of adult identity. While traditional film criticism often interprets music as an expression of mood, Analytical Phenomenological Psychology allows us to recognize a deeper function: music discloses the lived temporality through which ordinary existence is constituted.
 
From a Husserlian perspective, consciousness is intrinsically temporal, unfolding through the dynamic interplay of retention, primal impression, and protention. Human beings never inhabit a pure present; every moment contains preserved traces of what has just been experienced and anticipations of what is expected to occur. Rota's recurring musical motifs reproduce this structure with remarkable precision. Each theme carries the memory of its previous appearances while simultaneously opening a horizon of expectation. The listener therefore experiences the score as a continuous temporal flow in which past, present, and future interpenetrate. Giulietta's consciousness operates in exactly the same way: childhood memories, religious prohibitions, marital anxieties, fantasies, and unrealized possibilities coexist within a single experiential field.
 
The music becomes an audible analogue of consciousness itself.
 
Merleau-Ponty deepens this analysis by emphasizing that temporality is not an abstract framework but a bodily phenomenon. The body carries habits, memories, emotional dispositions, and anticipatory structures that silently organize perception. Musical repetition functions in a similar manner. The spectator does not simply recognize a recurring theme cognitively; the body remembers it pre-reflectively. Through rhythm, melody, and tonal recurrence, Rota creates what may be called an embodied temporality. His music becomes part of Giulietta's lived world, shaping the way reality is perceived before conscious interpretation emerges.
 
This phenomenological account converges with Bumbalo's Theory of Adult Ordinariness.
 
Ordinariness is not simply a social routine or a cognitive habit; it is fundamentally a temporal achievement maintained through repetitive meanings, recurring emotional rhythms, stable relationships, and narratives of continuity linking yesterday, today, and tomorrow. Adult identity persists because the future is expected to confirm the structures inherited from the past.
 
Ordinary existence is therefore a temporal organization of memory. Individuals inhabit not merely the present moment but a sedimented history of familiar meanings that provide coherence, predictability, and psychological stability.
 
Rota's score gives acoustic form to this temporal continuity. The recurring motifs of Juliet of the Spirits function as sonic sediments of memory. They return again and again, reproducing the stability characteristic of Giulietta's ordinary existence. Yet as the film progresses, these themes become increasingly transformed, displaced, fragmented, or recontextualized. What initially sounded familiar begins to acquire an uncanny quality. The listener encounters a world in which continuity persists but no longer guarantees certainty. Memory remains active, yet its capacity to organize experience begins to weaken.
 
From the standpoint of Analytical Phenomenological Psychology, this musical transformation represents the phenomenological onset of the Rupture of Ordinariness. Before Giulietta consciously recognizes the collapse of her marriage and identity, her temporal horizon has already begun to disintegrate. The future can no longer be anticipated through the familiar structures of the past. Rota's music therefore performs a unique psychological function: it allows the spectator to hear the collapse of ordinary temporality before the protagonist is able to understand it conceptually. The score becomes the acoustic manifestation of existential transformation.
 
The comparison with 8½ reveals the full significance of this process. In 8½, Guido's consciousness is characterized by temporal excess. Memories, fantasies, artistic projects, regrets, and imagined futures simultaneously invade the present. Rota's music mirrors this proliferation through circus-like motifs, ironic transitions, nostalgic melodies, and abrupt emotional shifts. Time becomes centrifugal, expanding in multiple directions at once. Guido inhabits a world in which ordinary temporality has already dissolved.
 
Giulietta's situation is fundamentally different. She begins within a stable temporal order governed by repetition, loyalty, habit, and predictability. Her drama is not the consequence of temporal fragmentation but of its progressive collapse. Accordingly, Rota's score in Juliet of the Spirits does not primarily express dispersion; it narrates the disintegration of certainty. If 8½ is the music of a consciousness already beyond the ordinary, Juliet of the Spirits is the music of a consciousness witnessing the loss of the ordinary in real time.
 
For this reason, Juliet of the Spirits may be interpreted as the most complete cinematic anticipation of Bumbalo's Theory of Adult Ordinariness. Through Rota's score, Fellini makes audible the temporal structure that sustains adult identity and the crisis that emerges when this structure can no longer maintain itself. The music reveals that authentic transformation begins when memory ceases to guarantee the future and consciousness becomes open to previously unimaginable possibilities of being. Rota thus becomes not simply the composer of Fellini's images but the composer of lived temporality itself, giving sound to the fragile continuity through which ordinary life is maintained, disrupted, and ultimately transcended.
 
6.  Feminine Subjectivity and Existential Freedom

The psychoanalytic and phenomenological dimensions of Juliet of the Spirits converge most powerfully in Fellini’s exploration of feminine subjectivity. Giulietta begins the film as a woman whose identity has been shaped almost entirely by external expectations: marital duty, religious morality, childhood conditioning, and social norms. Her self-understanding is mediated through the gaze of others — especially Giorgio, whose emotional absence paradoxically reinforces her dependence.

Fellini’s narrative does not present Giulietta as a passive victim. Instead, he constructs a
cinematic environment in which her interior life becomes the primary reality. Giulietta is not an object of representation; she is the subject of perception. The film privileges her consciousness, her sensory world, her emotional oscillations, and her imaginative horizon.

This shift anticipates themes later developed in feminist film theory. Laura Mulvey’s critique of the “male gaze” identifies how classical cinema often positions women as objects of visual pleasure.

Fellini, despite his own complex relationship with femininity, creates in Juliet of the Spirits an unusually rich portrayal of female interiority. Giulietta’s liberation does not depend on romantic fulfillment or external validation. It emerges from a transformation in her relation to herself.
Her journey resembles an existential awakening. Like the existential subject described by Sartre and Merleau-Ponty, Giulietta must assume responsibility for defining her own existence. 

She must confront the inherited structures — guilt, repression, obedience — that have shaped her identity and choose a new mode of being.

The film’s conclusion is deliberately open. Giulietta does not triumph in a conventional sense; she does not resolve her marriage, nor does she adopt a predetermined model of liberation.

Instead, she steps into a new relationship with her own experience. Her freedom is not a destination but a process — an ongoing reconfiguration of consciousness.

7.  Conclusion

Juliet of the Spirits stands as one of Federico Fellini’s most profound explorations of interior life. Through its fusion of dream imagery, symbolic figures, chromatic intensity, and psychological complexity, the film constructs a cinematic language capable of expressing the hidden
dimensions of human experience. Fellini does not merely depict Giulietta’s crisis; he visualizes the very processes through which consciousness organizes, distorts, and transforms reality.

From a psychoanalytic perspective, Giulietta’s journey reveals the intricate interplay between repression, desire, guilt, childhood conditioning, and unconscious forces. Her visions, apparitions, and symbolic encounters illustrate both Freudian mechanisms of return and
Jungian processes of individuation. The spirits that populate her world are not supernatural entities but autonomous psychic contents seeking integration. Giulietta’s liberation emerges through her willingness to confront these hidden dimensions of herself.

From a phenomenological perspective, the film dissolves the boundary between objective
reality and subjective experience. Fellini constructs a world in which perception, embodiment, memory, and imagination shape the very texture of existence. Giulietta’s crisis becomes a phenomenological rupture — a moment when the familiar world collapses and the structures of lived experience become visible. Her awakening consists in reclaiming her own perceptual and
emotional agency.

Ultimately, Giulietta’s transformation is neither a simple victory nor a definitive resolution.

Fellini refuses the comfort of closure. Instead, he presents self-discovery as an ongoing process: a continuous reconfiguration of consciousness, identity, and meaning. Giulietta’s final gesture — stepping into a new relation with herself — embodies a form of existential freedom grounded not in external change but in interior awakening.

More than sixty years after its release, Juliet of the Spirits remains a powerful meditation on identity, perception, femininity, and psychological transformation. It demonstrates cinema’s
unique capacity to visualize the invisible processes of the mind, offering a sensory and symbolic journey into the labyrinth of the self.
 
From the perspective of Bumbalo's Analytical Phenomenological Psychology, Giulietta's transformation acquires an even deeper significance. The film portrays the collapse of adult ordinariness and the emergence of a new existential configuration. What initially appears as marital suffering becomes a developmental rupture capable of exposing hidden dimensions of selfhood.
 
Fellini's masterpiece therefore illustrates a fundamental principle of the Theory of Adult Ordinariness: human beings often encounter authentic transformation only when the structures that guarantee ordinary psychological continuity begin to fail. The ordinary is revealed both as a shelter and as a prison.
 
Giulietta's final liberation does not consist in solving an interpersonal problem but in crossing a threshold of consciousness. She leaves behind not merely an unfaithful husband, but an entire mode of existence built upon dependency, conformity, and inherited meanings. What emerges is a more autonomous phenomenological subject capable of inhabiting uncertainty without surrendering authenticity.
 
Seen in this way, Juliet of the Spirits becomes not only a psychoanalytic and phenomenological film, but also an exemplary cinematic representation of Bumbalo's Theory of Adult Ordinariness and its model of transformative psychological development.

This article offers an integrated psychoanalytic and phenomenological interpretation of Federico Fellini’s Juliet of the Spirits (1965). Drawing on Freud, Jung, Husserl, Merleau‑Ponty, and Bumbalo’s Analytical Phenomenological Psychology (APP), the study examines Giulietta’s crisis as a collapse of adult ordinariness and a transformative rupture in the structure of lived experience. The film is interpreted as a cinematic visualization of unconscious dynamics, symbolic figures, embodied perception, and the phenomenological constitution of reality. Through color, space, dream imagery, and Nino Rota’s score, Fellini constructs a world where interiority becomes the primary reality. Giulietta’s liberation emerges not from external resolution but from a new relation to herself, revealing Fellini’s cinema as a meditation on identity, perception, temporality, and feminine subjectivity.
